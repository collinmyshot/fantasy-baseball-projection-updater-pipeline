SP Streamonator: Weight & Threshold Validation

Methodology Series · Appendix A — Empirical Calibration

The Streamonator collapses three signals — pitcher skill (SP Skillz), opponent offense (Team Rater), and ballpark environment (Park Factor) — into a single composite score. Two design choices drive how useful that score actually is: the relative weights given to each component, and the thresholds that divide the score range into actionable buckets. This appendix documents how both were calibrated against historical data.

The Good Start Metric

Every validation analysis here is grounded in a single outcome variable: the Good Start Score, a 0–4 integer that measures how well a start went for a fantasy manager. One point is awarded for each of the following criteria met in a given outing:

Criterion Threshold Rationale
Innings pitchedIP ≥ 5.0Minimum length for a fantasy-meaningful outing
StrikeoutsK ≥ floor(IP) − 1Roughly one strikeout per inning, one forgiven
Earned runs (sliding)IP ≥ 6: ER ≤ 2  |  IP 5–5.2: ER ≤ 3  |  IP 4–4.2: ER ≤ 2  |  IP < 4: ER ≤ 1ER threshold scales with depth; more runs are forgiven for longer outings
WHIPWHIP < 1.20Direct fantasy scoring component in most formats

A score of 3–4 is a “good” start from a fantasy standpoint — quality contribution on most scoring criteria. A 0–1 is a bad start (early exit, blown game, or both). The 2 tier is a middle ground: met most criteria but fell short in one key area. In the 22,917-start validation sample (2021–2025), the overall split is 54.5% good (3–4), 20.0% OK (2), and 25.5% bad (0–1).

The Composite Score Formula

Each component is expressed as an index centered at 100. The Team Rater and Park Factor are inverted before entering the formula (200 − index), so that a stronger opponent or more hitter-friendly park lowers the score. SP Skillz enters as-is. The composite is a weighted average:

Stream Score = (wSPZ × SPZindex) + (wTR × (200 − TRindex)) + (wPF × (200 − PFindex))
where wSPZ + wTR + wPF = 1

For the Team Rater sub-component specifically, the index fed into this formula blends two signals: the full-season standard TR index and the vs.-handedness split (vs. LHP or vs. RHP depending on the pitcher's arm), weighted 1:2. This was validated as optimal via grid search on the same historical sample — the handedness-split adds independent signal, especially for left-handed starters who face lineups that swing dramatically depending on platoon composition.

Validating the Outer Weights: 6:3:1 vs. 1:1:1

The two most natural weighting schemes to compare are 6:3:1 (SPZ-dominant), which reflects the intuition that pitcher skill is far more predictive than opponent or park, and 1:1:1 (equal weight), which treats all three inputs as equally informative. The validation used Spearman rank correlation between the composite score and the Good Start Score across all 22,917 starts.

6:3:1 weights — Spearman ρ = 0.2955 1:1:1 weights — Spearman ρ = 0.2523 Difference: Δρ = +0.0432 in favor of 6:3:1 Sample: 22,917 starts · 2021–2025

The gap is substantial. A +0.043 Spearman improvement over ~23,000 observations is not noise — it reflects the genuine reality that SP Skillz (pitcher talent) is the dominant predictor of whether a given start goes well. Team Rater and Park Factor add real information at the margin but cannot substitute for pitcher quality. Treating all three as equivalent dilutes the most informative signal.

Bucket Thresholds: 95/105 vs. 90/110

The composite score is translated into three action buckets: Start (score above threshold), Flip (borderline), and Sit (score below threshold). The thresholds determine how aggressively the model forces a recommendation vs. deferring to judgment.

Tighter thresholds (95/105) classify more starts as actionable — about 49% fall in the Start or Sit buckets, giving managers a clear lean on roughly half the schedule. Wider thresholds (90/110) are much more conservative, reserving the Start and Sit labels for only the most extreme scores (about 16% of starts), and routing the remaining 84% to the Flip/judgment zone.

The right trade-off depends on how you want to use the tool. Tighter thresholds are more useful for managers who want a larger set of actionable recommendations and are willing to accept more variance. Wider thresholds are appropriate when you only want high-confidence calls and are comfortable making most decisions independently.

Takeaway The 6:3:1 / 95–105 configuration (current defaults) wins on the metric that matters most for a weekly streaming tool: Spearman rank correlation with actual outcomes (+0.043 vs. equal weighting). It also routes nearly half of all starts to an actionable bucket while producing a meaningful 31.7-point gap in %Good between the Start and Sit groups. The wider 90/110 thresholds produce cleaner bucket outcomes in isolation, but 84% of starts in the Flip zone means the tool has essentially declined to make a recommendation for most scheduling decisions. The 6:3:1 / 95–105 defaults optimize the combination of predictive accuracy and operational usefulness.
